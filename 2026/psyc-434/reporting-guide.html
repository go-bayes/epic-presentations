PSYC 434: Conducting Research Across Cultures

Reporting Guide

This guide shows what to report in the research report. Use it to connect a clear psychological question to the causal estimand, analysis choices, results, and interpretation.

The main task is to define a psychological question clearly, then answer that question. Do not let the statistical workflow replace the question. Each table, figure, and paragraph should help the reader see what you asked, why it matters psychologically, what comparison you estimated, and what answer the evidence supports.

Scope of this guide

This guide is written mainly for the Option A final assignment, where the supplied scaffold covers average treatment effects, policy trees, and E-values. Option B students should still use the question-definition, identification, measurement, reporting, and sensitivity-analysis sections as a structure. Where Option B departs from the supplied scaffold, state the corresponding analysis choices and explain why they answer the same psychological question.

Who you are writing for

The "reader" throughout this guide is a non-specialist. For Option A, write for the readership of a psychological science journal: a professional audience that reads empirical psychology but has no background in comparative or causal-inference methods. For Option B, write for the Marsden EHB panel: scientifically literate assessors from outside your sub-field. In both cases, define every method-specific term at first use.

Minimum complete report

A complete report should include:

  1. Psychological question: the question, its psychological interest, and the causal estimand that answers it.
  2. Identification argument: the treatment, time zero, outcome, target population, adjustment set, and assumptions.
  3. Average treatment effect table: estimates, Bonferroni-adjusted confidence intervals, E-values, and sample sizes.
  4. Results interpretation: a direct answer to the psychological question, using the standardised outcome scale.
  5. Sensitivity analysis: E-values for Option A, or the corresponding sensitivity analysis for Option B.
  6. Policy-tree material for Option A: selected trees, graphing decisions, subgroup estimates, and the depth comparison appendix.

For Option B, replace item 6 with the analysis required by your question and assignment brief. Explain how that analysis answers the psychological question.

Start with the psychological question

Begin the report by stating the psychological interest of the question. A clear question names the population, treatment contrast, outcome, time frame, and reason the answer matters.

Use this structure:

Among [target population], how would [psychological outcome] change if investigators intervened to set [exposure/treatment] to [level $a=1$] rather than [level $a=0$], over [follow-up period]?

Then add one or two sentences explaining the psychological interest. For example, say whether the question concerns belonging, meaning, distress, social support, identity, emotion, behaviour, or another psychological process. This explanation should make clear why the treatment contrast and outcome belong together.

Outcome scale rule

The simulator's four wellbeing outcomes are pre-z-scored. Report effects in standard deviation (SD) units. Do not report original-scale 1–7 effects, because that scale is not available in the simulated outcomes.

The report should keep returning to this question:

  1. Methods: show how the design identifies the causal estimand implied by the question.
  2. Results: report the estimates needed to answer the question.
  3. Interpretation: explain what the estimates imply for the psychological question, within the limits of the identification strategy.

The ten-step causal inference checklist

Before reporting results, check that each piece is in place.

Steps 0–3: problem definition

  1. Psychological question and well-defined treatment. Specify the psychological question, the exposure, and the contrast (e.g., "weekly religious service attendance vs. less than weekly").
  2. Time zero. State when treatment assignment or initiation begins and when follow-up starts.
  3. Well-defined outcome. State the outcome measure, its scale, and when it was assessed.
  4. Target population. Define who the results apply to, including any weighting for population representativeness.

Steps 4–6: identification strategy

  1. Exchangeability. Describe the baseline covariates you adjust for and why they are enough, under your causal directed acyclic graph (causal DAG).
  2. Consistency. Explain why the treatment is well-defined and uniform across individuals.
  3. Positivity. Report checks showing that both exposure levels appear across the relevant covariate patterns.

Steps 7–9: implementation

  1. Measurement (Week 10 preview). Explain how the outcome is measured and any assumptions needed for the measure to mean the same thing across people, groups, and time.
  2. Attrition handling. The simulated data do not have missing responses, so follow the Lab 10 template: state how panel dropout would be handled if present, for example with inverse probability of censoring weights.
  3. Transparent reporting. Document the analysis choices and assumptions.

Target trial emulation

Frame your causal question as: "How would the psychological outcome change if we intervened to set everyone's exposure to level $a=1$ rather than $a=0$, conditional on baseline characteristics?"

Reporting average treatment effects

Required average treatment effect (ATE) table

Include these elements for each outcome:

OutcomeEstimate (SD units)Bonferroni 95% CIE-value (point)E-value (Bonferroni bound)
Outcome A0.12[0.05, 0.19]1.81.3
Outcome B0.15[0.08, 0.22]2.11.4

Key reporting elements

  1. Effect sizes: report in standard deviation units, following the outcome scale rule above.
  2. Confidence intervals: report the multiplicity-adjusted (Bonferroni) interval, since the design is outcome-wide across four outcomes.
  3. E-values: report two, one for the point estimate and one for the lower end of the multiplicity-adjusted confidence interval (the bound nearest the null). Both are produced by the ate_table() helper in the research-report template's setup.R.
  4. Sample size: total analysed after exclusions and weighting.
  5. Question connection: state how the estimate answers the psychological question posed at the start of the report.
  6. Practical interpretation: explain the size of the estimate without inventing a raw scale. For example, a 0.20 SD estimate is modest for one person, but may matter at population scale if the intervention is credible, low burden, or widely available.

Example results text, not a template

"Weekly religious service attendance was estimated to improve all four wellbeing outcomes. The largest estimates were for sense of belonging ($\beta = 0.18$ SD units, Bonferroni 95% CI: 0.11–0.25) and life satisfaction ($\beta = 0.15$ SD units, Bonferroni 95% CI: 0.08–0.22). The point E-values were above 1.6. The Bonferroni-bound E-values were above 1.3, meaning an unmeasured confounder would need risk-ratio associations of at least 1.3 with both attendance and these outcomes, above and beyond measured covariates, to push the multiplicity-adjusted lower bound to the null."

Reporting heterogeneous treatment effects

Option A

The policy-tree workflow is the only heterogeneity output. The rank-weighted average treatment effect (RATE) and Qini diagnostics introduced in Lab 8 are not part of this report's scaffold; the template does not ship code for them, and you do not need to include them.

For Option B, report the heterogeneity analysis that follows from your psychological question and available data. If you do not conduct a heterogeneity analysis, state that the report focuses on the overall causal estimand and explain why that estimand is the appropriate answer to the question.

Reporting policy tree results for Option A

Present each policy tree on the standardised outcome scale. The tree describes the action or high-response region implied by the supplied rewards; it does not force a fixed percentage treated.

The course policy-tree workflow uses an outcome-only objective. A do not treat leaf means the rule assigns the no-treatment action for that covariate profile. It does not compute money saved, staff time saved, or other resource savings. To make savings part of the analysis, investigators would need to specify a treatment cost in outcome units. They would then subtract that cost from the treatment reward and refit or compare trees across plausible cost values.

Selection and graphing workflow

Use this sequence:

  1. Fit both depth-1 and depth-2 policy trees.
  2. Compare held-out policy value.
  3. Select the depth-2 tree only when its point estimate improves held-out policy value by at least min_gain_for_depth_switch.
  4. Report the selected tree in the main text.
  5. Put the full depth comparison in the appendix.
  6. Apply the graphing rule.
  7. Keep all outcomes in the tables and prose, even when their trees are not graphed.

The course default for switching from depth-1 to depth-2 is:

min_gain_for_depth_switch <- 0.01

State this threshold before reporting the selected tree. If depth-2 clears the threshold but uncertainty is wide, describe the rule as promising or fragile. Use stability, equity, and implementation burden to temper the conclusion. The margot_policy_workflow() function applies the point-gain rule automatically and exposes the comparison in wf$best$depth_summary_df.

Apply margot::margot_select_grf_policy_trees() after the policy-tree workflow runs. The graphing rule keeps a policy tree only when both the policy-value lower confidence limit and the treated-uplift lower confidence limit exceed zero. Course defaults set both lower-confidence-interval thresholds at zero:

policy_value_lower_threshold <- 0
treated_uplift_lower_threshold <- 0

State these thresholds in your methods. The graphing rule is a precommitment device: state the test, then graph only what passes.

Subgroup reporting

For each graphed tree, report the high-response subgroups with their estimated effects, uncertainty, and sample proportions. Use the standardised scale.

Report coverage as the treated share implied by the selected rule, not as a budget constraint. If a programme can treat only a fixed share, say so explicitly. The default policy tree estimates the value of a shallow allocation rule; it does not solve a fixed-capacity allocation problem.

Example: high-response subgroups for life satisfaction

  1. Older adults with high baseline belonging (age > 45, baseline belonging > +1 SD)
    • Standardised effect: $\beta = 0.28$ SD units (95% CI: 0.21–0.35)
    • Sample proportion: 23%
  2. Younger adults with lower baseline purpose (age <= 45, baseline purpose < 0)
    • Standardised effect: $\beta = 0.22$ SD units (95% CI: 0.16–0.28)
    • Sample proportion: 31%
  3. All others
    • Standardised effect: $\beta = 0.08$ SD units (95% CI: 0.04–0.12)
    • Sample proportion: 46%

Example policy tree text, not a template

"The policy tree estimates the expected value of assigning the single treatment according to the fitted rule. In this example, the rule assigns treatment to two profiles with larger estimated gains in life satisfaction. Older adults (45+) with high baseline belonging had the largest estimated gain ($\beta = 0.28$ SD units). They represented 23% of the sample. Interpret these leaves as parts of an allocation rule, with age and baseline belonging used as splitting variables."

Avoid phrasing such as "the do-not-treat leaf saves resources" unless a treatment cost has been built into the objective. Under the course workflow, the accurate wording is: "the outcome-only rule assigns these profiles to no treatment because the no-treatment action has the higher estimated value for that leaf."

Sensitivity analysis: E-values

Interpretation

An E-value says how strong an unmeasured confounder would need to be, on the risk ratio scale, with both the treatment and the outcome to explain away the observed effect.

An E-value is a sensitivity analysis for unmeasured confounding. It does not prove that unmeasured confounding is absent.

There is no universal threshold at which an E-value becomes "safe". Interpret it against the study design, the covariates already measured, and plausible omitted causes in the setting. Report the E-value for the point estimate and for the confidence-limit closest to the null, and explain what kind of unmeasured confounder would be needed for the result to disappear.

Example sensitivity text

"For sense of belonging, the E-value was 2.4. An unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both religious service attendance and belonging by a risk ratio of 2.4 each, above and beyond the measured covariates, to explain away the estimate."

Methods section template

A complete methods section should include:

  1. Psychological question and causal estimand: the question, why it matters psychologically, and the causal comparison that answers it.
  2. Treatment definition: what the exposure is, how it is coded, and the contrast of interest.
  3. Time zero and follow-up: when assignment occurs, when follow-up starts, and why that timing matches the intervention.
  4. Outcome definition: measures used, timing of assessment, any transformations applied.
  5. Target population: sampling frame, weighting strategy, eligibility criteria.
  6. Causal identification: covariates adjusted for, with a justification for conditional exchangeability.
  7. Statistical analysis: estimation method, key tuning parameters (e.g., number of trees, minimum node size).
  8. Attrition handling: censoring weights, stages of dropout addressed.
  9. Heterogeneity assessment: policy-tree depth (with parsimony decision), policy value, treated uplift, and the graphing-rule decision. For Option B, describe the corresponding heterogeneity analysis if one is used.
  10. Sensitivity analysis: E-values for all primary estimates, or the corresponding sensitivity analysis for Option B.

Reporting checklist

Do report

  • Effect sizes in SD units (the simulator's outcome scale) with multiplicity-adjusted (Bonferroni) confidence intervals
  • Sample sizes after exclusions and weighting
  • E-values for both the point estimate and the Bonferroni-adjusted lower bound
  • Clear practical interpretation of effect sizes
  • Subgroup sizes and subgroup estimates for graphed policy trees
  • The parsimony threshold and the graphing-rule thresholds you used
  • Target trial framework and causal question
  • Explicit treatment and outcome definitions
  • A direct answer to the psychological question stated at the start of the report

Do not report

  • Model coefficients without interpretation
  • p-values alone without effect sizes
  • Original-scale (1–7) effects for the simulated outcomes; the simulator returns z-scored outcomes only
  • Technical details that obscure main findings
  • Causal claims beyond your identification strategy
  • Results that are disconnected from the psychological question

Figure presentation

ATE plots

  • Use forest plots with confidence intervals.
  • Order by effect magnitude or E-value.
  • Include sample sizes.

Policy tree plots

  • Show decision rules clearly.
  • Include sample proportions in each node.
  • Provide plain-language interpretation alongside the tree.
