This lab moves from evaluating heterogeneity (Lab 8) to making decisions across several outcomes at once. You will run a simplified teaching version of the lab's margot workflow on a four-outcome batch: estimate average treatment effects with E-values, test for genuine heterogeneity, fit depth-2 policy trees, evaluate targeting value with QINI and RATE, and read margot's auto-generated prose summary.

What you will learn

  1. How to read an outcome-wide average treatment effect (ATE) forest plot with E-values.
  2. How to test for genuine heterogeneity per outcome.
  3. How to read a depth-2 policy tree and the auto-generated prose that describes it.
  4. How to compare per-outcome QINI curves and RATE-AUTOC summaries.
  5. How to audit the workflow against simulator ground truth.

Connection to previous labs

Lab 5 introduced the average treatment effect. Lab 6 introduced the conditional average treatment effect (CATE). Lab 8 evaluated whether targeting based on a CATE ranking has practical value. This lab adds two new ideas: estimating across multiple outcomes at once and turning a CATE into a transparent allocation rule.

How this lab is different

Earlier labs fit one causal forest at a time, on one outcome, using grf directly. Today's lab uses margot to fit a batch of forests at once, attach E-values for sensitivity analysis, and produce decision-tree allocation rules with auto-generated prose. The script is a teaching analogue, not a reproduction of the full manuscript workflow. Production scripts add further steps: more outcomes, sample weights, adverse-outcome flipping, cross-validated heterogeneity interpretation, depth comparison, policy-value audits, and larger stability runs. Today's lab loads pre-fitted models from a cache (about 80 MB) so the exercise fits inside one session.

Cached fits

The cache holds three artefacts:

  • models_binary — the batch causal forest (one per outcome) with augmented inverse-propensity-weighted (AIPW) scores, out-of-bag predictions, and the combined ATE table.
  • policy_tree_stability — bootstrap-based stability output for each outcome's depth-1 and depth-2 policy trees.
  • policy_workflow — the interpretive layer: depth recommendations, plots, and auto-generated prose summaries.

The fit script that produced the cache is at scripts/fit-lab-09-cache.R. You do not need to run it during the lab; it is there so you can see exactly what was fitted.

Teaching simplification

The cache is deliberately smaller than the lab's research scripts. It is designed to teach the sequence of decisions, not to reproduce the full employer-gratitude analysis workflow.

Step 1: outcome-wide ATE forest plot

The combined ATE table holds one row per outcome with the risk-difference effect, a 95% confidence interval, and two E-values. The E-value of the point estimate measures how strong an unmeasured confounder would need to be, on the relative-risk scale, to explain the result away. The E-value bound applies the same calculation to the confidence-interval limit closest to the null.

print(models_binary$combined_table)

Convert the table into a forest plot:

ate_plot <- margot_plot(
  models_binary$combined_table,
  type = "RD",
  order = "magnitude_desc",
  e_val_bound_threshold = 1.2,
  label_mapping = label_mapping,
  save_path = tempdir()
)
print(ate_plot$plot)

Reading the forest plot

The dashed line at zero is the null. Estimates whose E-value bound is close to 1 are fragile: a relatively modest unmeasured confounder could explain the effect away. Treat those rows with caution when you describe the result. The plot's $interpretation slot contains a one-sentence draft naming the outcomes most worth treating as causal. Read it as a draft, not a verdict.

Step 2: omnibus heterogeneity test

The ATE asks "does it work on average?". The next question is "does it work the same for everyone?". margot_omnibus_hetero_test() wraps grf::test_calibration(). The output's "Differential prediction" coefficient is what matters: a positive coefficient with a small p-value means the forest sees genuine heterogeneity, not just sampling noise.

omnibus <- margot_omnibus_hetero_test(
  models_binary,
  label_mapping = label_mapping
)
print(omnibus)

What if heterogeneity is absent?

If an outcome shows reliable mean prediction but no differential prediction, the forest is telling you that targeting will not beat random allocation for that outcome. Reporting only the ATE is the honest move.

Step 3: per-outcome policy trees

A policy tree converts the personalised CATE into a transparent allocation rule: "treat people in this leaf, do not treat in this leaf". The lab caps depth at two, so each rule asks at most three yes/no questions before committing.

Two functions render each outcome's policy tree. margot_plot_decision_tree() returns the tree diagram alone — the rule as a slide-ready flowchart. margot_plot_policy_tree() returns the prediction-points scatter — the same rule shown as a partition of the underlying covariate space, with each individual coloured by the assigned action. Print both for every outcome:

model_ids <- names(label_mapping)
for (m in model_ids) {
  cat("\n=== ", label_mapping[[m]], " ===\n", sep = "")
  print(margot_plot_decision_tree(
    policy_tree_stability,
    model_name = m,
    max_depth = 2,
    label_mapping = label_mapping
  ))
  print(margot_plot_policy_tree(
    policy_tree_stability,
    model_name = m,
    max_depth = 2,
    label_mapping = label_mapping
  ))
}

Reading a policy tree

Each non-leaf node names a covariate and a threshold. Branches go left if the condition is true and right otherwise. Each leaf says "treat" or "do not treat". A leaf labelled "treat" with covariates that read like a real-world subgroup is what makes a policy tree useful: the rule can be defended in front of a clinician, teacher, or community organiser.

Step 4: QINI curves

QINI curves answer: if only a share of the population could be treated, would ranking people by estimated benefit beat random allocation? The vertical lines mark example treatment shares. This is a targeting diagnostic, not a budget-constrained policy-tree fit.

qini_plots <- margot_plot_qini_batch(
  models_binary,
  label_mapping = label_mapping,
  spend_levels = c(0.1, 0.4)
)
for (p in qini_plots) print(p)

A curve that bows above the diagonal at a 30% treatment share says the ranking has practical value at that share. A curve hugging the diagonal says targeting is no better than random.

Step 5: RATE table

RATE summarises targeting value as a single number per outcome. margot_plot_rate_batch() returns a list of per-outcome plots; margot::margot_rate() returns the underlying numbers as a table.

rate_plots <- margot_plot_rate_batch(
  models_binary,
  target = "AUTOC",
  label_mapping = label_mapping
)
for (p in rate_plots) print(p)

rate_table <- margot::margot_rate(
  models_binary,
  target = "AUTOC",
  label_mapping = label_mapping
)
print(rate_table)

AUTOC versus QINI

AUTOC weights all targeting rates equally. QINI weights mid-range rates more. AUTOC suits exploratory work where the budget is not fixed; QINI suits a real budget question with a known share treated.

Step 6: read the auto-generated prose

margot synthesises a draft narrative from the policy-workflow object. In the cached fit, that object was created with margot_policy_workflow() from the policy-tree stability output, the original analysis data, and the label mapping. The prose is generated from the same numbers you saw above; it does not bring new information. Its value is structural: it forces an assumption → finding → caveat shape that you can imitate when you write your own results section.

cat(wf$report_prose)

The policy_brief_df is a tabular one-pager for the same content:

print(wf$policy_brief_df)

Read the prose critically

Auto-generated text is a draft. Check the numbers against the tables you printed earlier. Replace any phrasing that overstates causal certainty. The prose is a starting point for your own writing, not a final answer.

Step 7: audit against simulator ground truth

The analyses above never see the truth. The simulator stores the true individual treatment effects in tau_community_<outcome> columns. Ranking outcomes by their true population mean tau lets you audit margot's recommendations without circularity.

d <- simulate_nzavs_data(n = 5000, seed = 2026)
d0 <- d |> filter(wave == 0)

true_tau_table <- tibble::tibble(
  outcome = c("Sense of Purpose", "Belonging", "Self-esteem", "Life satisfaction"),
  true_mean_tau = c(
    mean(d0$tau_community_purpose),
    mean(d0$tau_community_belonging),
    mean(d0$tau_community_self_esteem),
    mean(d0$tau_community_life_satisfaction)
  ),
  true_sd_tau = c(
    sd(d0$tau_community_purpose),
    sd(d0$tau_community_belonging),
    sd(d0$tau_community_self_esteem),
    sd(d0$tau_community_life_satisfaction)
  )
) |>
  arrange(desc(true_mean_tau))

print(true_tau_table)

The true_sd_tau column is the headroom for targeting: it tells you whether even a perfect ranking would deliver appreciable extra benefit beyond the average. Compare this ranking with the order in margot_plot()'s ATE forest. Discrepancies between estimated and true rank are an honest measure of how much information the forest extracted from a finite sample.

Ethical considerations

A high-value rule still needs judgement

A policy tree maximises expected treatment benefit under the objective you give it. Before anyone acts on it, ask:

  • Proxy variables. Does the tree split on variables correlated with protected characteristics such as ethnicity, gender, or socioeconomic status?
  • Fairness. Does targeting those who benefit most leave out people who would still benefit?
  • Transparency. Can a community organiser, teacher, clinician, or member of the public understand the rule?
  • Override. When should someone override the rule because they know relevant context the model cannot see?

When presenting policy tree results, discuss these trade-offs plainly.
