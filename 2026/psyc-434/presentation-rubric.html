PSYC 434: Conducting Research Across Cultures

Presentation Rubric (10%)

This rubric grades the in-class presentation in Week 12. The presentation is 10 minutes, with one panel question afterwards. You may use the whiteboard and paper notes; no slides, handouts, or devices.

The talk is judged on four criteria, each weighted equally (2.5% per criterion). Each criterion is scored in one of four bands:

  • A range — 80–100% of the criterion mark.
  • B range — 65–79%.
  • C range — 50–64% (the pass band).
  • D / E — below 50% (fail).

These bands map to Te Herenga Waka — Victoria University of Wellington's grade letters. For the full schedule, see the VUW grades and grade point average page.

The four criteria are independent: a talk can be excellent on causal reasoning and developing on "so what".

What we look for

A good presentation makes a non-specialist audience walk out understanding (i) what causal question you asked, (ii) how you propose to answer it, and (iii) why the answer would matter. The rubric below operationalises that.

Rubric

1. Clarity and structure (2.5%)

How readable is the talk? The criterion covers pacing, whiteboard work, and signposting.

BandDescriptor
A rangeOpens with a one-sentence motivation a non-specialist can follow. Whiteboard work is uncluttered, legible, and used purposefully (not as a backdrop). Pacing fits the 10-minute window with time to spare. Each section flows into the next without backtracking.
B rangeStructure is followed; the audience can track where you are. Whiteboard work supports the talk but contains some clutter or unclear notation. Pacing slips slightly (under-time or rushing the close).
C rangeAudience can mostly follow but has to reconstruct the order. Whiteboard work is hard to read or under-used. Pacing forces the speaker to skip sections or run over.
D / ETalk is hard to follow. Whiteboard is a wall of text or barely used. Speaker runs out of time before reaching the answer.

2. Causal reasoning (2.5%)

Does the talk handle the causal logic correctly? This criterion is course-specific and carries weight equal to clarity.

BandDescriptor
A rangeCausal question is well-defined: target population, exposure with a clear contrast, and outcome(s) all named precisely. The causal estimand is stated (e.g. average treatment effect) and distinguished from the statistical estimand. The DAG identifies a sufficient adjustment set; identification assumptions (consistency, positivity, conditional exchangeability) are named and defended.
B rangeQuestion, exposure, and outcome are clear. DAG is present and largely correct. Identification assumptions are mentioned; one assumption is glossed or imprecise. Estimand is stated but not always distinguished from the statistical quantity.
C rangeCausal question is implicit rather than stated. DAG is present but missing key edges or confounders. Identification language is inconsistent (e.g. "predicts" used for a causal claim).
D / EQuestion is conflated with the statistical model. DAG is missing or incorrect. Causal language is undisciplined throughout.

3. So what — including subgroup and ethics (2.5%)

Why should anyone outside the room care? This is the criterion most talks under-serve. Because the course studies comparative work, your "so what" should also identify the subgroup for which the treatment effect is strongest (from your policy tree) and one ethical consideration that would shape deployment.

BandDescriptor
A rangeNames the population the result speaks to, the magnitude that would count as decision-relevant, the decision-maker who could act on it, and the assumption that, if false, would change the recommendation. Identifies the strongest-response subgroup in plain language and notes one equity, proxy-variable, or Te Tiriti consideration that bears on deployment.
B rangeNames a plausible audience and decision. Magnitude is mentioned but not anchored to a real-world unit. The strongest-response subgroup is named but the ethical consideration is gestured at rather than spelled out.
C rangeAsserts importance without naming who acts on the result. Subgroup or ethics treated as an aside. "So what" is generic ("this is important for wellbeing") rather than specific.
D / E"So what" is absent or replaced by a statement that the topic is interesting. No subgroup or ethics content.

4. Response to the panel question (2.5%)

How well does the speaker handle one challenge after the talk? You may ask one brief clarifying question before answering.

BandDescriptor
A rangeListens to the question, restates it briefly to check understanding, and gives a direct answer. Where the answer is uncertain, the speaker says so and names what evidence would resolve it. The response engages the substance of the challenge rather than restating prepared material.
B rangeAnswers the question asked. Some restating of prepared material; some hedging. Acknowledges uncertainty where present.
C rangePartially answers; speaker drifts into adjacent prepared material. Uncertainty is hidden rather than acknowledged.
D / ECannot engage the question. Repeats earlier slides without addressing the challenge, or answers a different question.

A worked "so what" answer

For a talk on religious_service → outcome-wide wellbeing:

"If our estimate is right, encouraging weekly attendance among adults who currently attend less often would lift four wellbeing outcomes by roughly a tenth of a standard deviation each. For a public-health body deciding whether to fund community programmes that lower the cost of regular religious participation, that magnitude is in the range that has justified investment in similar programmes elsewhere. The recommendation depends on conditional exchangeability after adjusting for thirteen baseline covariates plus baseline outcomes; if a strong unmeasured confounder remained — say, a stable disposition we have not measured — the result could be reversed for some outcomes. The E-values say a confounder would need to be associated with both attendance and the outcomes by a risk ratio of at least 1.6 to do that."

This answer names: the population (adults who currently attend less often), the magnitude (a tenth of a standard deviation, four outcomes), the decision-maker (a public-health body), the decision (whether to fund community programmes), and the assumption that would change the answer (conditional exchangeability, with an E-value benchmark).

Common pitfalls

  • Spending the first three minutes on background and never reaching the estimand.
  • Drawing the DAG without naming the adjustment set it implies.
  • Reporting only standardised effects with no anchor to a decision.
  • Reading from notes verbatim instead of using them as a prompt.
  • Treating the panel question as a hostile attack rather than an invitation to clarify.

Practical checklist

Preparation

  • Rehearse the opening sentence until it lands cleanly.
  • Plan the whiteboard layout in advance (left third for the DAG, centre for the estimand, right for the result).
  • Time yourself: aim for eight minutes, leaving two for breathing room.
  • Anticipate one likely panel question and prepare a one-sentence answer.
  • Re-read the Reporting Guide for terminology consistent with the course.
